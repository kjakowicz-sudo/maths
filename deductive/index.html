Three moves. That is the whole game.

  1. Cross off the row and the column. Look along the ? square's row and down its column. Every symbol you see is ruled out. If only one symbol survives, that is your answer.
  2. Fill an easier square first. If more than one survives, find a different empty square you can work out, pencil it in, and look again. One new symbol in the right place is often all it takes.

1 The one rule What the grid is, and what you are actually asked to do

Start here

If you have ever done a sudoku, you already know this test. And this is the easier cousin of it.

A grid. A handful of symbols. One rule:

No symbol appears twice in the same row, or twice in the same column.

That's it. There are no 3×3 boxes to worry about, no arithmetic, and — this is the part people miss — you do not have to fill in the grid. One square is marked with a ?, and your only job is to say which symbol belongs there.

Everything else on the screen is just material for working it out.

Why it feels harder than it is

Two reasons, and neither is about ability.

The first is that the symbols are meaningless shapes, so there is nothing to hold on to. A sudoku has numbers, and you can say "I need a 7 here". Here you have to say "I need the… the star-ish one", and that is harder to keep in your head.

The second is the clock. It's a short, brisk test, and the squares you need to look at are scattered.

Both are fixed by the same thing: a fixed order of looking, and using the pencil. More on both shortly.

What you can do that most people don't

You can fill in other squares as you go. The real test lets you place symbols in the empty squares, and so does this app — tap an empty square and pencil something in.

People skip this because it feels like extra work. It isn't: it's how you stop holding five things in your head at once. Writing one symbol down frees up all the attention you were spending remembering it.

2 Move 1 — cross off the row and the column The move that solves most grids on its own

The idea

The ? square sits in one row and one column. Those two lines are the only things that constrain it.

So: run your eye along the row and note every symbol already in it. Those are all impossible — the rule forbids a repeat. Then run your eye down the column and do the same.

Whatever is left standing is the answer.

Doing it without losing your place

  1. Look at the strip of symbols underneath the grid. That is the full set, and it never changes.
  2. Go along the row first, and mentally knock out each symbol you meet from that set.
  3. Then go down the column and knock out the rest.
  4. Name what survives.

Always row first, then column, every time. Not because the order matters logically — it doesn't — but because having a fixed order means you never lose track of where you had got to.

Worked through

When this works It works whenever the row and column between them contain every symbol but one. On a 4×4 grid that's common, and it is the fastest mark in the test. Always try it first, even when the grid looks busy.
Watch out Checking the row and forgetting the column, or vice versa. If two symbols are still standing after move 1, you haven't failed — you've just learned that this grid needs move 2.
3 Move 2 — fill an easier square first What to do when two symbols are still standing

The idea

Sometimes move 1 leaves you with two or three possibilities. The instinct is to stare harder at the ? square. That never helps, because you have already taken everything that square can tell you.

The information you need is somewhere else in the grid.

So go and get it: find a different empty square that move 1 does solve, pencil that symbol in, and come back. You have now added a symbol to a row and a column — and if you picked well, one of them is the ? square's.

Which square to go for

Not any old empty square. Pick one that is in the same row or the same column as the ?. Those are the only squares whose answer can change what the ? square sees.

Among those, go for the one in the fullest line — the row or column with the fewest gaps. The fuller the line, the more likely move 1 cracks it straight away.

Worked through

The habit worth building When move 1 fails, don't re-read the ? square. Ask instead: which other empty square in this row or column can I solve? That one question turns a stuck grid into a solved one more often than anything else in this app.
4 Move 3 — "nowhere else to go" The one that feels like a trick until it clicks

A different way of asking the question

Moves 1 and 2 ask: what can go in this square?

Move 3 turns it round and asks: where can this symbol go?

Here's the reasoning. Take a row. Every row must end up containing all the symbols — that's the rule. So pick a symbol the row is still missing, and ask where in that row it could possibly sit. Check each empty square in turn: does that square's column already contain the symbol? If it does, it can't go there.

Knock out enough squares and you may find the symbol has exactly one place left. Then it must go there — even though that square, looked at on its own, might have had several possibilities.

Why it's worth the effort

Because it finds answers the other moves can't. A square with three candidates looks hopeless from the inside; from the symbol's point of view it can be forced.

It is also the move that the harder grids are built around. If you've done moves 1 and 2 and you're still stuck, this is almost always what's wanted.

Worked through

Watch out You can run this on a column just as well as a row — pick a symbol the column is missing and ask which of its empty squares could take it. Same logic, turned ninety degrees. People forget the column version and miss easy marks.
5 Using the pencil How to stop holding five things in your head

Why this matters more than it sounds

The thing that actually runs out on this test is not cleverness. It is working memory — the small number of things you can hold in mind at once. Most people can hold about four, and these grids will happily ask you for six.

The moment you feel yourself thinking "so that one was the star… no, wait" — that's working memory overflowing. It is not a sign you can't do it. It is a sign you are doing it in your head when you don't have to.

What to do

  • The moment you deduce a square, place it. Don't hold it while you look at the next thing.
  • Pencil in anything you are sure of, even if it isn't the ? square. Every symbol you place makes the grid easier.
  • Don't try to finish the grid. You are not being marked on it. Place only what helps the ?.
  • Only rows and columns matter. Diagonals mean nothing here, however tempting the pattern looks.

In this app, tap any empty square to pencil a symbol in, and tap again to change it. Your pencil marks show faded, so you can always tell them from the symbols the puzzle gave you.

The honest version People who are good at this aren't holding more in their heads than you. They're holding less, because they write things down sooner.
6 Traps The mistakes that cost marks

1. Answering with a symbol that is already in the row

It sounds impossible, and it is the single most common error under time pressure — you check the column carefully, and your eye never goes back along the row.

The fix: before you commit, one last sweep. Is my answer anywhere else in this row? In this column? Two seconds.

2. Trying to solve the whole grid

You only need one square. Filling the rest is unpaid work, and on a 5×5 it will eat your clock.

The fix: only pencil in squares that sit in the ?'s row or column. Everything else is scenery.

3. Believing in diagonals

The rule covers rows and columns. Not diagonals, not corners, not pretty patterns. Your eye will find patterns anyway — ignore them.

4. Losing your place halfway

You get three symbols crossed off, something distracts you, and you start again. Twice. Now the clock has gone.

The fix: the fixed order — row first, then column — and the pencil. Both exist to make interruptions survivable.

5. Freezing on a hard one

Some grids need move 3 and several steps, and they are genuinely harder. The test may well be feeding you harder ones because you are doing well.

The fix: decide your limit in advance. Run moves 1, 2 and 3 once each. If nothing, pick the most likely survivor and move on.

7 Working at speed What the clock actually rewards

What this test is like

Short and brisk, like the rest of the Aon suite — a few minutes, a run of grids, and it keeps serving them up. It is often adaptive: get them right and the grids get harder.

Two things follow from that, and they're both reassuring.

You are not meant to find them all easy. If the grids are getting harder, that is the test telling you that you are doing well. The difficulty is a scoreboard, not a verdict.

Nobody finishes feeling they aced it. An adaptive test is designed to push you to the edge of what you can do. Feeling stretched at the end is the normal, expected experience — including for the people who score highest.

A rhythm that works

  • Move 1 immediately, every time. It's ten seconds and it solves a good share of grids outright.
  • If two survive, go straight to move 2. Don't re-read the ? square.
  • Pencil as you go. Never hold a deduction in your head while you look elsewhere.
  • Check your answer against the row and column before committing. Two seconds, and it kills the most common error.
  • Have a give-up rule. Moves 1, 2, 3, once each — then best guess and on.

How to train for it

Untimed and gentle until the three moves are automatic. Then mixed. Only then timed. Going to timed early just teaches you to panic.

Ten minutes a day. Grid puzzles improve fast — faster than almost anything else you could revise — because you're building a habit, not learning facts.

The summary One rule, three moves, and a pencil. There is nothing else in this test.

Tips

Short and practical. Mostly about the clock and about not panicking.

1. Always open with move 1

Row first, then column, crossing symbols off the set as you go. It takes about ten seconds, needs no cleverness, and solves a large share of grids on its own.

Even when the grid looks complicated, do this first. It either hands you the answer or tells you the grid needs more, and both are useful.

2. Check your answer before you commit

One sweep along the row, one down the column. Is the symbol you are about to pick already sitting in either of them?

That two-second check removes the most common wrong answer in the whole test.

3. Place things, don't remember them

The limit on this test is working memory, not reasoning. The instant you work a square out, put the symbol in it. Holding it while you look somewhere else is how deductions get dropped.

People who look fast at this are mostly just writing things down sooner.

4. Only work on squares that can help

You are asked for one square, not the grid. The only other squares worth filling are the ones sharing a row or column with the ? — they're the only ones that can change what it sees.

Solving the far corner is unpaid work.

5. Getting harder means doing well

These assessments are usually adaptive. If the grids grow from 4×4 to 5×5, or start needing several steps, that is the test responding to a good run.

So the feeling of "this is getting hard" is not a sign it's going badly. It is evidence of the opposite, and it's worth knowing that in advance so it doesn't rattle you on the day.

6. Have a give-up rule, decided now

Moves 1, 2 and 3, once each. If the answer hasn't appeared, pick the best survivor and move on.

A rule made in advance is a plan. The same decision made mid-test is panic. One grid is cheap; two minutes stuck on it is not.

7. On the day

  • Laptop, not phone. The squares are small and you'll be moving between them constantly.
  • Do the practice tasks the publisher gives you — they show you the exact interface, which removes a whole layer of surprise.
  • One quiet uninterrupted go. You get one attempt, and this is a test where losing your place is expensive.
  • Paper next to you is still worth having, even with an on-screen grid.

And remember what it actually is: a sudoku with no boxes, where you only have to fill one square.

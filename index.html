1 Percentages — the foundation What "%" actually means, and how to find a percentage of something

Start here: a comparison

Imagine a test with exactly 100 marks. You get 37 marks. Everyone would say you scored 37 per cent.

That's all a percentage is: a score out of 100. Nothing more clever than that.

The word helps too. Per cent means per hundred. It's the same "cent" as in century (100 years) and centimetre (100 of them make a metre). So "37%" is just a shorter way of writing "37 out of every 100".

What it is

Real life doesn't come in neat hundreds. A class might have 25 people. A salary might be £28,400. A percentage takes whatever size the thing is and re-describes it as if it were out of 100.

That's the whole point of percentages: they put different-sized things on the same scale so you can compare them fairly. 18 out of 25 and 68 out of 100 are hard to compare as they are. As percentages — 72% and 68% — it's obvious in a second.

Two words worth pinning down now, because every question uses them:

  • The whole — the full amount, the thing being cut up. The total class, the full price, the entire budget.
  • The part — the bit of it you're talking about.

The method — finding a percentage of a number

There are two ways. Learn the first one for understanding, use the second one for speed.

Way 1 — the 1% brick. If 1% means "one hundredth", then:

1% of a number = the number ÷ 100

Once you know what 1% is worth, you can build any percentage you like by multiplying. Want 15%? That's 15 of those bricks.

Way 2 — the decimal. 15% is 15 hundredths, which is 0.15. So "15% of 240" is just "0.15 × 240". One button-press on a calculator. (Chapter 2 explains where 0.15 comes from.)

Worked example — slowly

What is 15% of 240?

Step 1 — find 1%. Divide by 100. Dividing by 100 moves the decimal point two places to the left, so 240 becomes 2.4.

240 ÷ 100 = 2.4   (so 1% of 240 is 2.4)

Step 2 — build up to 15%. We have one brick, and we need fifteen of them.

2.4 × 15 = 36

Step 3 — answer the question that was asked. 15% of 240 is 36.

Check it makes sense Let's get there a completely different way. 10% of 240 is 24 (just divide by 10). 5% is half of 10%, so 12. Add them: 24 + 12 = 36. Same answer, so it's right. It's also comfortably smaller than 240, which it has to be — 15% is only a small slice.

The method — "what percentage is X out of Y?"

This is the other question you'll be asked. The recipe:

part ÷ whole × 100

Divide first, then multiply by 100 to turn it into a percentage.

39 out of 60 applicants passed. What percentage passed?

Step 1 — decide which number is the whole. 60 is everybody, so 60 is the whole. 39 is the part. The whole goes on the bottom of the division.

Step 2 — divide the part by the whole.

39 ÷ 60 = 0.65

That 0.65 is the answer already — just in decimal form, out of 1 rather than out of 100.

Step 3 — turn it into a percentage. Multiply by 100.

0.65 × 100 = 65%
Check it makes sense 39 is a bit less than 40, and 40 out of 60 is two thirds, which is about 67%. Our answer, 65%, sits just under that. Good. And because the part is smaller than the whole, the answer had to be under 100% — it is.

The trap

Watch out The single most common slip is dividing the wrong way round: 60 ÷ 39 instead of 39 ÷ 60. That gives 1.538, which would be 153.8% — more than everybody. Before you press the button, ask yourself: which number is the whole? The whole always goes on the bottom. And if the part is smaller than the whole, your answer must be under 100%.

Quick reference

  • 1% = divide by 100
  • 10% = divide by 10
  • 50% = halve it
  • 25% = halve it, halve it again
  • 20% = divide by 5 (or double 10%)
  • 5% = half of 10%
  • Any percentage can be built from 10% + 5% + 1% pieces. 35% = 10 + 10 + 10 + 5.
  • "What % is X out of Y" → X ÷ Y × 100
2 Fractions, decimals and percentages Three ways of saying exactly the same thing

Start here: a comparison

Think about money for a second. You can say "fifty pence", or "£0.50", or "half a pound". Three different ways of writing it. Nobody is confused, and nobody thinks they're different amounts.

Fractions, decimals and percentages work exactly like that. ½ and 0.5 and 50% are the same amount wearing different clothes.

What it is

Here's the one idea that makes all of this click:

  • A fraction is a division waiting to happen. ¾ literally means "3 divided by 4".
  • A decimal is that division already done. 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75.
  • A percentage is that same decimal, scaled up so it's out of 100 instead of out of 1. 0.75 → 75%.

So they are three stops on the same road. You can walk in either direction.

Why does a test care? Because questions deliberately mix them. "Two fifths of staff" and "40% of staff" mean the same thing, and the question that mentions both is testing whether you noticed.

The method

Four small moves. That's genuinely all of it.

Going from…to…Do this
FractionDecimalDivide the top by the bottom
DecimalPercentageMultiply by 100
PercentageDecimalDivide by 100
PercentageFractionPut it over 100, then simplify

"Multiply by 100" and "divide by 100" both just move the decimal point two places — right when multiplying, left when dividing. 0.375 → 37.5. And 37.5 → 0.375.

Worked example — slowly

3 out of 8 trains were late. Write that as a percentage.

Step 1 — write it as a fraction. 3 out of 8 is 3⁄8. The whole (8) goes on the bottom.

Step 2 — turn the fraction into a decimal. The fraction line means "divide".

3 ÷ 8 = 0.375

Step 3 — turn the decimal into a percentage. Multiply by 100, which slides the point two places right.

0.375 × 100 = 37.5%

So 37.5% of the trains were late.

Check it makes sense 3 out of 8 is a bit less than 4 out of 8, and 4 out of 8 is a half — 50%. So the answer should be a bit under 50%. 37.5% is. If you'd got 3.75% or 375%, the decimal point slipped.

The trap

Watch out Seeing 0.4 and thinking "4%". It isn't — it's 40%. Here's why: 0.4 means four tenths, and four tenths is forty hundredths. Percentages are hundredths, so 0.4 = 40%. The rule that saves you: the point always moves two places, not one. 0.4 → 40%. 0.04 → 4%.

3 Percentage change How much something went up or down, in fair terms

Start here: a comparison

A jumper is reduced by £10. A sofa is reduced by £10. Same money off — but one of those feels like a real bargain and the other barely registers.

Why? Because £10 off a £40 jumper is a quarter of the price, while £10 off a £800 sofa is almost nothing. The size of a change only means something next to what you started with.

Percentage change is the tool that does that comparison for you.

What it is

Percentage change answers: "compared to where it started, how big was the move?"

One word matters more than any other here: the original. That's the value you started from — the older one, the "before" one, the one that came first in time. Everything gets measured against it.

The method

  1. Find the change. Bigger number minus smaller number. Note whether it went up or down.
  2. Divide the change by the original. Not by the new one. By the one you started with.
  3. Multiply by 100 to turn it into a percentage.
percentage change = (change ÷ original) × 100

Worked example — slowly

A shop sold 250 units last year and 310 units this year. What was the percentage increase?

Step 1 — which one is the original? Last year came first, so 250 is the original. This is the step people rush. Don't.

Step 2 — find the change.

310 − 250 = 60

Sales went up by 60 units.

Step 3 — compare that change to the original. Divide by 250, not by 310.

60 ÷ 250 = 0.24

Step 4 — make it a percentage.

0.24 × 100 = 24%

So sales rose by 24%.

Check it makes sense Work it forwards. 10% of 250 is 25, so 20% is 50, and 4% is 10. Together, 24% of 250 = 60 — exactly the change we found. It fits, so the answer is right.

The trap

Watch out — trap 1 Dividing by the new number. Using 310 above gives 19.4%, which is one of the wrong options you'll be offered. Say it out loud before you divide: "compared to what it was".
Watch out — trap 2 Percentage points are not percentages. If an interest rate goes from 2% to 3%, that's a rise of 1 percentage point — but it's a 50% increase (the change, 1, divided by the original, 2). Both are correct; they answer different questions. Read which one is being asked.
Watch out — trap 3 Up 50% then down 50% does not bring you back. £100 up 50% is £150; £150 down 50% is £75. The second percentage was taken from a bigger number, so it took more away.

Quick reference

  • change ÷ original × 100 — the original is the "before" number
  • Went up → say "increase". Went down → say "decrease". Don't report a negative percentage unless asked.
  • A change bigger than the original means over 100% — that's allowed and often correct (doubling = a 100% increase).
  • From 2% to 3% = 1 percentage point = a 50% increase.
4 Multipliers The one-step shortcut for adding or taking off a percentage

Start here: a comparison

Think of an old photocopier with a zoom dial. Set it to 110% and everything comes out 10% bigger. Set it to 85% and everything comes out 15% smaller. You don't work out the extra and glue it on — the dial does it in one pass.

A multiplier is that dial, written as a number.

What it is

A multiplier is a single number you multiply by to apply a percentage change in one move.

The thinking behind it: the thing you start with is 100% of itself. Always. So…

  • Add 20% and you now have 100% + 20% = 120% of the original, and 120% as a decimal is 1.2.
  • Take off 15% and you're left with 100% − 15% = 85%, which as a decimal is 0.85.

That's the whole trick. Work out what percentage you're left holding, then turn it into a decimal by dividing by 100.

Why bother, when you could find the amount and add it on? Two reasons. It's one calculator step instead of three, so there's less to get wrong. And it's the only sane way to handle chapter 5 (working backwards).

The method

  1. Increase by p% → multiplier is (100 + p) ÷ 100. Up 8% → 108 ÷ 100 → ×1.08
  2. Decrease by p% → multiplier is (100 − p) ÷ 100. Down 8% → 92 ÷ 100 → ×0.92
  3. Multiply the starting amount by it. Done.

Notice the shape of them: an increase always gives a multiplier above 1, and a decrease always gives one below 1. That's a free check every single time.

Worked example — slowly

A laptop costs £480. In a sale it is reduced by 15%. What is the sale price?

Step 1 — what are we left holding? We start at 100% and lose 15%.

100% − 15% = 85%

Step 2 — turn 85% into a decimal. Divide by 100 (point moves two places left).

85 ÷ 100 = 0.85   (this is our multiplier)

Sense-check the multiplier before using it: it's below 1, and the price is going down. Correct shape.

Step 3 — one multiplication.

£480 × 0.85 = £408

The sale price is £408.

Check it makes sense The slow way: 10% of 480 is 48, and 5% is 24, so 15% off is 48 + 24 = £72 off. 480 − 72 = £408. Same answer. And £408 is a bit below £480, which is exactly what a 15% discount should look like — a small bite, not half the price.

The trap

Watch out — trap 1 Multiplying by 0.15 instead of 0.85. That gives £72, which is the discount, not the price. Both numbers will be sitting there in the multiple-choice options. Ask yourself what the question wanted: the money off, or the money you pay?
Watch out — trap 2 Writing 5% as 1.5. It's 1.05. Five per cent is 0.05, not 0.5. If it helps, always write the percentage as a three-digit thing first: 105% → 1.05.
Watch out — trap 3 Two changes in a row: multiply the multipliers, don't add the percentages. Up 10% then up 10% is ×1.1 ×1.1 = ×1.21 — a 21% rise overall, not 20%.

Quick reference

  • +5% → ×1.05  ·  +10% → ×1.1  ·  +20% (VAT) → ×1.2  ·  +2.5% → ×1.025
  • −5% → ×0.95  ·  −10% → ×0.9  ·  −25% → ×0.75  ·  −33% → ×0.67
  • Increase → multiplier above 1. Decrease → multiplier below 1. No exceptions.
  • Doubling is ×2, which is a 100% increase. Halving is ×0.5, a 50% decrease.
5 Reverse percentages You know the price after the change — what was it before?

Start here: a comparison

A jumper goes in a hot wash and comes out 20% smaller. You're holding the shrunken jumper. What size was it before?

Your instinct says "add 20% back on". But that's wrong, and it's worth seeing exactly why. The 20% that was lost was 20% of the original, bigger jumper — not 20% of the sad little one in your hands. Adding 20% of the small one back on doesn't return the same amount that was taken away.

So you can't nudge forwards. You have to properly undo the change.

What it is

These questions give you the value after something happened and ask for the value before.

You can spot them by their language. Look for: "a price including VAT", "after a 12% pay rise", "the sale price is", "what was it originally?", "what was the price before…". The number you're given is the finished one; the missing number is the starting one.

This is the topic that catches the most people out in real tests — not because it's hard, but because the wrong method feels so natural. Once you have the rule below, it becomes one of the easiest marks available.

The method

Remember from chapter 4: applying a percentage change means multiplying.

original × multiplier = final amount

Here you have the final amount and want the original. Multiplication undoes with division. So:

original = final amount ÷ multiplier
  1. Work out the multiplier exactly as before. (VAT at 20% added → 1.2. A 30% discount → 0.7.)
  2. Check the direction. Going forwards you multiply; coming backwards you divide.
  3. Divide the amount you were given by the multiplier.
  4. Check by going forwards again — multiply your answer by the multiplier and see if you land on the number in the question.

Worked example — slowly

A coat costs £96 including VAT at 20%. What was the price before VAT was added?

Step 1 — which number am I holding? £96 is the price with VAT already on it. The word "including" tells me it's the finished number. So this is a backwards question.

Step 2 — find the multiplier. VAT adds 20%, so the price went from 100% to 120% of itself.

120 ÷ 100 = 1.2

Step 3 — divide, don't multiply. The £96 is the "after". To get back, undo the multiplication.

96 ÷ 1.2 = 80

The price before VAT was £80.

Check it makes sense Run it forwards: £80 × 1.2 = £96. That's exactly the number in the question, so £80 must be right. This forwards check takes five seconds and catches every mistake in this topic — always do it.

The trap

Watch out Taking 20% off the £96. That gives £76.80, and it will absolutely be one of the options. It's wrong because the VAT was worked out on £80, not on £96 — the VAT added was £16, not £19.20. The give-away: if you then add 20% back to £76.80 you get £92.16, not the £96 you started with. It doesn't come back, so it wasn't right.

Quick reference

  • Forwards (before → after): multiply by the multiplier.
  • Backwards (after → before): divide by the multiplier.
  • Signal words for backwards: including, after, originally, before the increase, the sale price is.
  • Undoing a rise gives a smaller answer. Undoing a discount gives a bigger one. Check that before you move on.
  • Always verify by multiplying your answer forwards.
6 Ratios Sharing things out in proportion

Start here: a comparison

Making orange squash. The bottle says "one part squash to four parts water".

It doesn't tell you to use cups, or litres, or bucketloads — and it doesn't need to. It's telling you the recipe: however much squash you pour, use four times as much water. One cup and four cups. Or two cups and eight cups. The drink tastes identical either way, because the proportion is the same.

That recipe, written as 1 : 4, is a ratio. That's it. That's the whole concept.

What it is

A ratio tells you how a total is split between two or more people, or things, without telling you the actual amounts.

The two dots mean "to". So 3 : 5 is read out loud as "three to five", and it means "for every 3 of the first thing, there are 5 of the second".

Here's the idea that unlocks nearly every ratio question: think in parts. A ratio of 4 : 5 means the pile is cut into 4 + 5 = 9 equal parts. One person gets 4 of them, the other gets 5. Once you know what one part is worth in real money, you can build anything.

The method

For "share £X in the ratio a : b":

  1. Add the numbers in the ratio. That's the total number of parts.
  2. Divide the total amount by the number of parts. That gives the value of one part. This is the step that does the real work.
  3. Multiply the value of one part by each side of the ratio.
  4. Add your answers back up and check they come to the total you started with.

Worked example — slowly

£360 is shared between Amy and Ben in the ratio 4 : 5. How much does each get?

Step 1 — how many parts altogether?

4 + 5 = 9 parts

So think of the £360 as being cut into 9 equal piles.

Step 2 — what is one part worth? Divide the money by the number of parts.

£360 ÷ 9 = £40 per part

Step 3 — give everyone their parts. Amy has 4 parts, Ben has 5.

Amy: 4 × £40 = £160 Ben: 5 × £40 = £200
Check it makes sense Add the two shares back together: £160 + £200 = £360. That's exactly the amount we started with, so nothing has been lost or invented. Also, Ben's number is bigger than Amy's — which it should be, because 5 is bigger than 4. Two checks, both pass.

A second shape of question

Staff and managers are in the ratio 7 : 2. There are 56 staff. How many managers?

Step 1 — match the number to its side. The 56 lines up with the 7, because both refer to staff.

Step 2 — find one part. If 7 parts are worth 56 people:

56 ÷ 7 = 8 people per part

Step 3 — build the other side. Managers are 2 parts.

2 × 8 = 16 managers
Check it makes sense Is 56 : 16 really the same recipe as 7 : 2? Divide both by 8: yes, 7 : 2. And there are far fewer managers than staff, which matches a ratio of 7 to 2.

The trap

Watch out — trap 1 Dividing by one of the ratio numbers instead of by the total parts. £360 ÷ 4 = £90 is a tempting but meaningless number. Always add the sides together first.
Watch out — trap 2 A ratio is not a fraction. Squash mixed 1 : 4 is one fifth squash, not one quarter. The 4 is "parts of water", not "the whole drink". When a question switches to fraction language, rebuild it from parts: 1 part out of 5 total parts.
Watch out — trap 3 Answering the wrong half. If the question asks "how much more does Ben get?", the answer is £200 − £160 = £40, not £200. Re-read the final sentence before you commit.

Quick reference

  • Total parts = add the sides. One part = total amount ÷ total parts.
  • Simplify a ratio by dividing both sides by the same number: 20 : 30 → 2 : 3.
  • 2 : 3 and 4 : 6 and 10 : 15 are all the same ratio.
  • Ratio a : b means the first thing is a/(a+b) of the whole.
  • The shares must always add back up to the total. If they don't, something slipped.
7 Rates — speed and price per unit Anything with the word "per" in it

Start here: a comparison

You're in a supermarket holding two bags of rice. One is 2 kg for £3.00, the other is 5 kg for £7.00. Which is better value?

You can't compare them as they are — different prices and different sizes. So you do what everyone does instinctively: work out what one kilo costs in each. £1.50 a kilo versus £1.40 a kilo. Now it's obvious.

That "cost of exactly one" is a rate. You already use them every day.

What it is

A rate is how much of one thing you get for one of another thing. The word per always means "for each one".

  • 60 miles per hour = 60 miles for each one hour
  • £1.40 per kilo = £1.40 for each one kilo
  • 18 per cent = 18 for each one hundred (yes — percentages are rates too)

And the way you find any rate is always the same: divide by however many units you had. £7.00 for 5 kg → 7 ÷ 5 = £1.40 for 1 kg.

The method

To find a rate: divide the total by the number of units. "£7 for 5 kg" → 7 ÷ 5.

To compare value: put both options into the same "per one" and then compare. Cheaper per unit = better value.

For speed, distance and time, there are three versions of one relationship:

speed = distance ÷ time distance = speed × time time = distance ÷ speed

If you only remember one, remember distance = speed × time — the other two are that same sentence rearranged. Or reason it out from the units: miles per hour is "miles divided by hours", which tells you the formula without memorising anything.

Worked example — slowly

A train travels 150 miles in 2 hours 30 minutes. What is its average speed in miles per hour?

Step 1 — sort the time out first. This is where the marks are lost. "2 hours 30 minutes" is not 2.30 hours. Minutes have to be turned into a fraction of an hour by dividing by 60.

30 ÷ 60 = 0.5, so the time is 2.5 hours

Step 2 — pick the right version of the formula. We want miles per hour — miles for each one hour — so we divide the miles by the hours.

150 ÷ 2.5 = 60

Step 3 — write it with the units. The average speed is 60 miles per hour. A number without its unit is only half an answer.

Check it makes sense Run it forwards. At 60 mph, in 2 hours you'd cover 120 miles, and in the extra half hour another 30. 120 + 30 = 150 miles. That's the distance in the question, so 60 mph is right. And 60 mph is a believable train-ish speed — if you'd got 6 or 600, something went wrong.

The trap

Watch out — trap 1 Typing 2 hours 30 as 2.30. It's 2.5. Minutes divided by 60, always. 2h 15m = 2.25 h. 1h 45m = 1.75 h. 40 minutes = 0.667 h.
Watch out — trap 2 Dividing upside down. 2.5 ÷ 150 gives 0.0167, which is hours per mile — a real thing, but not what was asked. Let the unit name guide you: "miles per hour" literally says miles ÷ hours.
Watch out — trap 3 With value-for-money questions, the cheaper per unit wins — but check whether the question asks which is better value or how much you'd save. They want different numbers.

Quick reference

  • Minutes into hours: 15 min = 0.25 · 20 min = 0.333 · 30 min = 0.5 · 40 min = 0.667 · 45 min = 0.75 · 90 min = 1.5
  • distance = speed × time — rearrange for the other two
  • Price per unit = total price ÷ number of units
  • "Per" = "for each one" = divide by the number of them
  • Always write the unit next to your answer: mph, £/kg, per hour. It stops half the mistakes.
8 Currency conversion Swapping one money for another

Start here: a comparison

A bureau de change board says £1 = €1.17. Read that as a price tag: "one pound buys you one euro and seventeen cents".

It's shopping, really. If one pound buys 1.17 euros, then ten pounds buys ten lots of that, and a hundred pounds buys a hundred lots of it.

What it is

An exchange rate is just a swap deal written as a sentence about one unit. It's a rate, exactly like chapter 7 — euros per pound.

Everything becomes easy once you write the rate down as a full sentence with the "1" in it:

£1 = €1.17

Now you can see which way round things go, instead of guessing.

The method

  1. Write the rate as a sentence starting with the single unit: "£1 = €1.17".
  2. Decide the direction. Going from the currency on the "1" side (pounds) to the other one → multiply. Coming back the other way → divide.
  3. Do the one calculation.
  4. Sense-check the size using the rate. Since one pound is worth more than one euro here, an amount in euros should always be a bigger number than the same amount in pounds.

That last step is the one that saves you, and it works without you having to remember any rule at all. Ask: "should this number get bigger or smaller?" If your answer went the wrong way, you multiplied when you should have divided.

Worked example — slowly

The rate is £1 = €1.17. How many euros do you get for £240?

Step 1 — the rate as a sentence. One pound gets me 1.17 euros.

Step 2 — direction. I'm starting with pounds, and pounds are the "1" side, so I have 240 lots of "1.17 euros". That's a multiplication.

240 × 1.17 = 280.80

Step 3 — write it as money. €280.80. Two decimal places, because it's money.

Check it makes sense Euros are worth a bit less than pounds here, so I should end up with a bit more euros than the 240 pounds I put in. 280.80 is a bit more than 240. It fits. (If I'd divided by mistake I'd have got about €205 — fewer euros than pounds, which would be the wrong shape.)

Now backwards: the rate is £1 = €1.15. You have €287.50. How many pounds is that?

Step 1 — direction. I'm coming back to the "1" side, so this is a division.

287.50 ÷ 1.15 = 250

That's £250.

Check it makes sense Go forwards to test it: 250 × 1.15 = 287.50. Lands exactly on the number in the question. And pounds are worth more, so the pounds number should be smaller than the euros number — 250 is smaller than 287.50.

The trap

Watch out — trap 1 Multiplying when you should divide. Both answers look plausible on their own, which is why the size check matters so much. Decide before you calculate whether the number should grow or shrink.
Watch out — trap 2 Fees and commission. If there's a £5 fee taken before the exchange, take it off first, then convert what's left. Read carefully which currency the fee is in and whether it comes off before or after.
Watch out — trap 3 Rounding early. Keep the full number on your calculator until the end, then round to 2 decimal places for money.

Quick reference

  • Always rewrite the rate as "1 of this = that much of the other".
  • From the "1" currency → multiply. Back to it → divide.
  • Check by going forwards: your answer × rate should return the number in the question.
  • Money answers get 2 decimal places.
  • Weaker currency = bigger number. If £1 buys €1.17, the euro figure is always the larger one.
9 Reading tables and charts Finding the right number before you do any maths

Start here: a comparison

A table is a seating plan. Every number is sitting in a seat, and the seat is described by two labels: the row it's in and the column it's in.

Nobody finds seat 14F by scanning the whole plane. You find row 14, then you go along to F. Tables work the same way, and doing it in that order — deliberately, with your finger or your eye — removes almost all the errors in this topic.

Because here's the thing most people don't realise: in a real test, data questions are rarely hard maths. The maths is usually a percentage or a subtraction. The difficulty is entirely in picking up the right number in the first place.

What it is

Data interpretation gives you a table or a chart and asks a question that needs one or two numbers out of it. The test is measuring whether you can locate, read and use information accurately under time pressure.

Two things carry hidden meaning and are the source of most lost marks:

  • The units line. A heading like "Revenue (£000s)" means every number in that table is in thousands of pounds. A 48 in the table is £48,000. You'll also see (£m), (millions), (%), (per 1,000 people).
  • The scale on a chart. The gridlines tell you what the bars are worth. If labelled lines go 0, 20, 40, 60, then each line is 20 — and a bar halfway between 40 and 60 is 50.

The method

  1. Read the title. Five seconds. What is this actually about?
  2. Read the units. Out loud in your head: "these are thousands of pounds". Write "×1000" in the margin if it helps.
  3. Read the question and find the exact numbers you need. Row first, then column. Say the label to yourself as you land on it.
  4. Write those numbers down before calculating. On paper, away from the table. This is what stops you drifting into the next row.
  5. Do the maths — usually a percentage, a difference, a total or a ratio.
  6. Re-read the question and check you answered that, in the units it asked for.

Worked example — slowly

Using the table below: what percentage of the total Q1 revenue came from the North branch?

Revenue by branch (£000s)
BranchQ1Q2
North120138
South180171

West105121

The trap

Watch out — trap 1 Ignoring the units line. If that question had asked "how much revenue did North take in Q1?", the answer is £120,000 — not £120. The £000s heading is small, easy to skim past, and deliberately placed.
Watch out — trap 2 Reading the neighbouring row or column. Especially on a phone or a small screen. Q1 and Q2 sit right next to each other for a reason. Find the row, then track across carefully.
Watch out — trap 3 Confusing "percentage of the total" with "percentage change". "What share did North have?" divides by the total. "How much did North grow?" divides by North's own earlier figure. Two different wholes. Read the question twice.

Quick reference

  • £000s → the real figure has three more zeros. £m → six more.
  • Percentages of a total are unaffected by the units — but amounts are.
  • Chart scale: find two labelled gridlines, subtract, and you know what each gap is worth.
  • "Share of total" → divide by the total. "Growth" → divide by the earlier figure.
  • Write the numbers down before calculating. Every time.

One last thing

If a chapter didn't land the first time, that isn't a verdict on you — it usually means one earlier idea hadn't settled yet. Chapters 1 and 2 hold up everything else, so they're the ones worth re-reading.

Understanding comes from doing it a few times, not from reading it perfectly the first time. Nobody gets this from one pass.

Tips

Exam technique, which is a different skill from maths — and a much quicker one to fix.

1. Nearly all your wrong answers will be slips, not gaps

When people review their practice honestly, the pattern is almost always the same: they knew the method, and they mistyped a number, used the wrong one of two figures, or answered a slightly different question from the one asked.

That matters because slips and gaps need opposite fixes. A gap needs study. A slip needs slowing down and writing the steps out. If you're getting questions wrong but nodding along to the worked solution, you don't have a knowledge problem — you have a speed-and-layout problem, and that's very fixable.

Practical version: keep a pen and paper next to you, even for the easy ones. One line per step. Never hold two steps in your head at once.

2. Sanity-check the size of every answer

Before you move on, spend three seconds asking: is this number roughly the right size?

  • A discount should make the price smaller. A rise should make it bigger.
  • A part of a whole must be under 100%.
  • A share from a ratio must be less than the total, and the shares must add back up to it.
  • An average must land between the smallest and biggest values.
  • If 10% of something is 24, then 15% has to be around 36 — not 3.6, not 360.

Most wrong answers aren't slightly wrong, they're ten times wrong or upside down. A size check catches those instantly, and it costs you almost no time.

3. Read the units and the scale before you read the question

Tables and charts hide their most important information in the smallest text: £000s, millions, %, per 1,000 people, excluding VAT, Q1 only.

Make it a habit: title, then units, then question. A "48" under a £000s heading is £48,000, and the option "£48" will be sitting there waiting for you.

Then check the units your answer needs. Pounds or thousands of pounds? Miles or kilometres? Per week or per year? The maths can be perfect and the answer still marked wrong.

4. Don't round until the very last step

If a calculation gives 23.6842105, keep it. Use it in the next step. Only round when you write the final answer.

Rounding early is how you end up with an answer that's nearly one of the options but not quite — and then lose a minute wondering which one to pick.

Unless told otherwise: money to 2 decimal places, percentages to 1, and match whatever precision the answer options are using.

5. Calculator for the arithmetic, your head for the set-up

Cappfinity assessments let you use a calculator, and you should. But be clear about the division of labour:

  • Your job: decide what to calculate. Which number is the whole? Is this forwards or backwards? Do I divide by the total parts or by one side?
  • The calculator's job: the actual multiplying and dividing.

A calculator will happily give you a beautifully precise wrong answer. It cannot tell you that you divided by the new figure instead of the original.

So: work out the plan first, say it to yourself in words ("change divided by the original, times a hundred"), then pick up the calculator.

6. Pacing — Cappfinity scores speed and accuracy

This sounds stressful, but read what it actually means: rushing is not rewarded. A fast wrong answer scores nothing. A steady right answer scores fully. Speed only helps you once accuracy is there.

So the target isn't "be fast". It's be steady, and don't stall. A calm, consistent rhythm of around a minute to a minute and a half per question beats a pattern of sprinting, panicking and re-doing.

  • Give each question one clean attempt at full attention.
  • If you're 20 seconds in and you don't know where to start, that's information — make your best decision and move on rather than freezing. One lost question is cheap; five minutes lost on it is not.
  • Don't re-check work you've already checked once. Second-guessing costs more marks than it saves.
  • Timed practice is for building the rhythm. Untimed practice is for building the method. You need both, and method comes first.

Sova assessments are often adaptive — they adjust the difficulty as you go, and some sections are timed per question. The habits are identical: read the units, set the calculation up properly, check the size.

7. Multiple choice: work it out before you look at the options

The wrong options aren't random. They are deliberately built from the classic mistakes — the discount instead of the price, the division done upside down, the figure with 20% taken off instead of divided by 1.2.

That means a plausible-looking option is no evidence you're right. Get your own answer first, then go looking for it in the list.

If your answer isn't there, don't grab the nearest one. Go back and check the set-up — usually you've divided by the wrong number, or missed a units line.

8. If your mind goes blank

It happens, and it isn't a sign of anything. Blankness is nearly always the body reacting to pressure, not the brain failing to know something.

A reset that works: breathe out slowly, then do the smallest possible concrete thing — write down the two numbers in the question. Not the method, just the numbers. Almost always, the next step reappears once something is on the paper.

And remember what an aptitude test actually is: a few percentage and ratio questions in unfamiliar clothes. You have met every idea in it before.

9. What to do between now and the test

  • Short and daily beats long and occasional. Ten minutes a day will move you further than two hours on a Sunday.
  • Start untimed until a topic feels automatic, then switch that topic to timed.
  • Use the Progress tab to find your weakest topic and drill that one on its own.
  • When you get one wrong, don't just read the answer — find the exact step where your version and the worked version came apart. That single step is the thing to learn.
  • On the day: paper and pen, calculator within reach, read the units first. Same as practice.

Being out of practice and being bad at something are not the same thing, and they don't feel different from the inside. Only one of them is true here.

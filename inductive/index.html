1 What this test actually is And why staring at it doesn't work

Start here

A string of fairy lights is hanging in a window and one bulb has blown. You spot it immediately. Nobody taught you how. You didn't do any maths.

That is this entire test. Nine small pictures, eight of them following the same rule, one that doesn't. Find it.

What it is called, and what it is for

You'll see it named inductive reasoning, logical reasoning, diagrammatic reasoning, or — if the employer uses Aon's tests — scales ix. Same thing in each case: nine boxes, find the one that breaks the pattern.

Inductive just means working out the rule from examples, rather than being told it. You do it when you notice a friend only ever texts back late at night, or that the bus is always late on a Monday. Nobody told you the rule. You spotted it from the pattern.

Employers use it because it doesn't depend on what you were taught at school. There's no vocabulary to have missed and no formula to have forgotten. That is genuinely good news for you.

Why it feels impossible at first

Because the shapes are meaningless. A fairy light is a thing you know; a grey pentagon with two dots is not. So your eye has nothing to grab, and the natural response is to stare at the whole grid at once, waiting for the answer to jump out.

Sometimes it does. When it doesn't, staring harder never helps — and that moment of blankness is where people conclude they "can't do these". They can. They just haven't been given a method yet.

Chapter 2 is the method. It is not complicated, and it works every time.

Worth knowing Nothing in this test is about intelligence in the way it feels like it is. It's a trained skill with a small number of rules behind it, and people improve at it quickly — usually within a few sessions of knowing what to look for.
2 The method The one habit that changes everything

Start here: a comparison

You've lost your keys. You could stand in the middle of the room and look around hopefully. Or you could check: pockets, then the table, then the sofa, then the coat.

The second one finds the keys. Not because it's clever, but because it's ordered — you never check the same place twice and you never miss a place entirely.

These puzzles work the same way. Staring is standing in the middle of the room. The checklist is checking your pockets.

The two ideas that make it work

Idea one: look for what the eight share, not what one has.

This sounds like the same thing and it really isn't. If you hunt for "the weird one", every box looks a bit weird — they're all slightly different from each other. But if you ask "what do most of these have in common?", you get a clean answer, and then the odd one is simply whatever doesn't have it.

So the question in your head should be: what is true of nearly all of them?

Idea two: check one feature at a time, in a fixed order.

Don't look at a box. Look at one feature across all nine boxes. Count the shapes in every box, one after another. Then forget counting and look only at colour. Then only at size.

This is the bit that feels wrong and is right. You are deliberately ignoring almost everything, on purpose, so that the one thing you're looking at has nowhere to hide.

The checklist

  1. Count — how many shapes are in each box?
  2. Shape — are they all the same kind?
  3. Fill — solid or hollow?
  4. Colour — any box using a colour no other box uses?
  5. Size — is one box's shapes bigger or smaller?
  6. Direction — is anything pointing or leaning differently?
  7. Position — inside, outside, top, bottom, spread out, touching?
  8. Relationship — does one feature depend on another? (The number of dots matching the number of corners, say.)

It's in that order for a reason: the top of the list catches most puzzles, and counting is the fastest thing you can do. Start at 1 every single time, even when you think you've already spotted something.

The checklist is available while you practise — there's a button for it under every puzzle. Use it openly at first. After a week or two you'll find you're running it without looking.

What it looks like in practice

Here is a real puzzle. Try to find the odd one before you read on — and use the list: count first.

The point You didn't need to be clever there. You needed to count. That's the whole skill: not insight, just an ordered search that you finish.
3 Count, shape and fill The first three checks — and most of your marks

Why these three come first

They are the easiest things to see and the most commonly used. If you only ever learned these three, you would already get a decent number of these puzzles right.

1. Count

How many separate shapes are in each box? Say the numbers under your breath as you go: "four, four, four, five — there."

Count everything, including a big outline shape that other things sit inside. People forget the container and miscount.

2. Shape

Are they all circles? All triangles? One box using something else?

Careful here, because shapes can be rotated. A square balanced on its corner is still a square, even though it looks like a diamond. Count the corners if you're not sure — corners don't change when something turns.

3. Fill

Solid (coloured in) or hollow (just an outline)? This is a favourite, because your eye reads a hollow shape as "lighter" rather than as "different", and skims past it.

The trap Stopping at the first difference you notice. Boxes differ from each other in all sorts of ways that are not the rule — the puzzle is built that way on purpose. A real answer is a feature where eight boxes agree and one doesn't. If two or three boxes share the thing you spotted, it isn't the rule. Keep going down the list.
4 Size, direction and position The next three checks

4. Size

Is everything in one box noticeably bigger or smaller than everywhere else?

Size is slippery because a bigger shape also looks "closer" or "bolder", and your brain reports that as a feeling rather than as a fact. Compare one box directly against its neighbour rather than trusting the impression.

5. Direction

Is anything pointing, leaning or facing a different way? Arrows and triangles are the usual culprits.

Useful trick: pick one feature of the shape — the tip of the arrow, the flat edge of the triangle — and ask where that one thing is pointing in each box. Up, right, down, left.

6. Position

Where are things in relation to each other? The common version: a small shape sitting inside a big one in every box, except one where it has slipped outside, or is overlapping the edge.

Other versions: everything hugging one side; one shape always on top of another; things touching versus separate.

The trap Treating "it's in a different spot" as automatically the answer. Positions wobble about in nearly every puzzle as deliberate noise. Position only counts as the rule when it's a relationship — inside versus outside, touching versus apart — not just "a bit further left".
5 Rules about the arrangement When no single shape is odd, but the layout is

The step up

So far the rule has been about the shapes themselves. Now it becomes about how they sit together. Nothing in the box is odd on its own — it's the arrangement that is.

This is the point where people who've only been pattern-spotting start to struggle, and where the checklist earns its keep: items 7 and 8 exist precisely for this.

The odd-one-inside rule

Every box contains several identical shapes and exactly one that's different — a little marker. The odd box out has no marker at all, or has two.

So you're not looking for a different shape. Every box has one of those. You're counting how many different shapes each box has.

Symmetry

Symmetrical means: fold it down the middle and the two halves match. Like a butterfly, or a human face, or the letter A.

In these puzzles, eight boxes are arranged so they'd fold neatly in half, and one is slightly off — something shifted, so the halves no longer match.

How to check it quickly: imagine a vertical line down the centre of the box and ask whether the left side is the mirror of the right.

How to spot this family If you've been down the whole checklist on count, shape, fill, colour, size and direction and found nothing, that's your signal: the rule is almost certainly about arrangement. Stop looking at individual shapes and start looking at the shape of the group.
6 Hidden rules Even and odd, and things that have to match

The hardest kind, and they're still only two ideas

In every puzzle so far, eight boxes looked the same in some way. In these, no two boxes look the same at all — and the rule is still there, one level down.

Even or odd

The counts are all different: this box has 2 shapes, that one has 6, another has 4. Counting gets you nowhere, because no count repeats enough to look like a rule.

But every one of those numbers is even — and one box has 5. That's the rule: not how many, but even or odd.

Even means it divides into two equal halves with nothing left over: 2, 4, 6, 8. Odd means one is left over: 1, 3, 5, 7. If you can pair the shapes up with none spare, it's even.

The signal for this one: you counted, and the numbers were all over the place. Don't conclude counting failed. Ask what the numbers have in common.

Things that have to match

This is the one that feels like a magic trick until you've seen it once.

Each box has a big shape and some dots. The number of dots is always the same as the number of corners on the big shape. Triangle, three dots. Square, four dots. Pentagon, five dots.

Every box is different from every other box — different shape, different number of dots. There is nothing to spot by looking. The only thing they share is the agreement between the two, and in one box it's broken.

Checklist item 8 is exactly this: does one feature depend on another? When two things in a box both vary, check whether they vary together.

Be realistic about these Hidden rules are the hardest items and they are a minority. Under time pressure, if you've run the whole list and nothing has appeared, make your best guess and move on. A minute spent on one of these costs you two easy ones later.
7 Traps that catch everyone The mistakes that aren't about ability

1. Grabbing the first difference you see

You notice box 6 is the only one with a shape near the bottom corner, and you click it. But three other boxes also have shapes near corners — you just didn't check.

The fix: before clicking, finish the sentence "all the others have…" out loud. If you can't finish it, you haven't found the rule yet.

2. Colour shouting over everything else

Colour is the loudest feature on the screen, so a box in a different colour pulls your eye whether or not colour is the rule. Puzzles deliberately scatter colours around as noise.

The fix: colour is only the answer when one box uses a colour no other box uses. Two red boxes among blue ones is not a rule, it's decoration.

3. Thinking a rotated shape is a different shape

A square on its corner looks like a diamond. A triangle upside down looks like a new shape entirely. They aren't.

The fix: count corners. Corners survive rotation. Three corners is a triangle however it's turned.

4. Expecting the odd one to look wrong

It usually doesn't. It is normally the most ordinary-looking box in the grid. The rule-breaking is often the absence of something — the only box without a hollow shape, the only one that isn't symmetrical.

The fix: remember you're testing a rule, not hunting for ugliness.

5. Restarting from scratch every few seconds

Under pressure people check count, drift to colour, lose their place, go back to count. Three passes, no progress, and the clock gone.

The fix: one pass, in order, finishing each item before the next. If the list runs out, guess and move on. You can always come back if there's time.

6. Treating a blank as a verdict

Everyone gets puzzles where nothing appears. Everyone. It means the rule is one of the hidden kinds, or you're tired — it does not mean anything about you.

The fix: have a rule for it in advance. Mine would be: run the list once, and if nothing lands, pick the box that feels least like the others and move straight on.

8 Working at speed What changes when the clock is running

What these tests actually ask of you

They're short and brisk. You get a stack of puzzles and a tight time limit, and nobody is expected to finish every one perfectly. The score comes from how many you get right in the time, so the winning approach is a steady rhythm with no stalls — not sprinting, and definitely not perfectionism.

Check the instructions on the day for how wrong answers are treated. If there's no penalty for a wrong answer — which is usual — then never leave one blank. An unanswered box scores nothing; a guess might score.

A rhythm that works

  • Count first, always. It's the fastest check and it catches more puzzles than anything else.
  • Give each puzzle one clean pass. Down the list, no doubling back.
  • Set yourself a stopping point. Something like: if I've run the list and nothing's there, I guess and move on. Decide this now, not in the moment.
  • Don't re-check an answer you've already committed to. Second-guessing costs more marks than it saves.
  • Expect a few to be unsolvable for you on the day. That's the design. Losing one is fine; losing two minutes to it is not.

How to train for it

Untimed first, until the checklist runs by itself. Only then switch to timed. If you go timed too early you'll practise panicking, which is a real skill and not one you want.

Short and daily beats long and occasional — ten minutes a day will move you further than an hour at the weekend. This sort of noticing improves faster than almost anything else you could revise.

The honest summary Speed on these comes almost entirely from the checklist becoming automatic. Nobody gets fast by trying to go faster. They get fast because they stopped having to think about where to look next.

Tips

Short, practical, and mostly about what to do when it isn't going well.

1. Count first. Every single time.

It takes three seconds, it needs no insight, and it catches more of these puzzles than any other check. Even when you're sure the answer is something else, count first — it either hands you the answer or rules out a whole category in one move.

Count the big outline shapes too. Forgetting the container is the most common miscount there is.

2. Finish this sentence before you click

"All the others have…"

If you can finish it, you've found the rule and your answer is safe. If you can't, you've found a difference, which is not the same thing — and the puzzle is full of those on purpose.

This one habit will remove more wrong answers than anything else on this page.

3. Look at one feature across nine boxes, not nine features in one box

The instinct is to study each box in turn until one of them seems wrong. That's slow and it rarely works, because boxes are designed to differ from each other in unimportant ways.

Instead: count across all nine. Then colour across all nine. You're sweeping the grid one layer at a time, and the rule has nowhere to hide.

4. Decide your give-up rule now

Before you sit the test, decide exactly what you'll do when a puzzle won't crack. A good one: run the checklist once; if nothing lands, pick the box that feels least like the rest and move on.

Made in advance it's a plan. Made during the test it's panic. The difference matters more than it sounds.

If the instructions say wrong answers aren't penalised — which is normal — never leave one blank.

5. Steady beats fast

These tests are scored on how many you get right in the time. That rewards a consistent rhythm, not a sprint. Sprinting produces the one thing that actually costs you marks: clicking before you've finished the sentence in tip 2.

Think of it as a brisk walk you can keep up, not a dash you can't.

6. When your mind goes blank

It will happen, and it means nothing about you. Blankness is pressure, not ability.

The reset: breathe out, then do the smallest concrete thing — count the shapes in the first box. Not solve it. Just count one box. Starting anywhere beats staring everywhere, and the method usually restarts itself once your eyes are moving.

If it stays blank: guess, move on, let it go. One puzzle is cheap.

7. Practise in short daily goes

Ten minutes a day will do more than two hours on a Sunday. This is pattern-noticing, and it settles overnight — little and often is how it sticks.

Stay untimed until the checklist runs without you thinking about it. Then switch to timed for a week before the real thing.

Use the Progress tab to see which rule types you keep missing. That's the most useful information in this whole app — it tells you which checklist item your eye skips.

8. On the day

  • Do the practice questions properly, even if you're impatient. They tell you the exact format.
  • Big screen if you have one. These are small pictures and a laptop beats a phone.
  • Check whether you can go back to earlier puzzles — it changes whether skipping is safe.
  • First one feels hard? Normal. Everyone's first one feels hard.

And remember what you're being asked to do: find a blown bulb in a string of fairy lights. You've done that your whole life.
